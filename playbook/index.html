Before you change anything

Three systems do the work. When customers churn, escalations pile up, or renewals surprise you, one of these is usually missing or disconnected from the others. Build them once, then run everything through them.

Lifecycle architecture

Defines what success looks like at every stage, from first contact through renewal, and who owns each handoff so no customer falls between teams.

Operational governance

Escalation tiers, severity definitions, and SLAs that keep issues from aging quietly in a queue, with clear ownership from first touch to resolution.

Value reporting

The metrics, dashboards, and business reviews that prove ROI to customers and connect customer experience to revenue for your executive team.

The workflow, in order

Seven steps to stand up or rebuild a customer operation. Work through them in sequence. Your progress saves in this browser.

Run a baseline diagnostic

Before redesigning, measure what is actually happening. Pull twelve months of churn, renewal, ticket, and satisfaction data, then talk to the people closest to the problem.

What you need: CRM and ticketing exports, renewal history, five customer conversations, five frontline conversations
More detail
  • Segment churn by reason, tenure, and customer size. Averages hide the story.
  • Find the oldest open tickets. Aging backlog is the fastest signal of broken ownership.
  • Ask frontline staff where they work around the process. Workarounds show you where the design failed.
1

Map the customer lifecycle

Define each stage with entry criteria, exit criteria, an owner, and the handoff to the next team. If a stage has no owner, it has no outcome.

What you need: Your stage list, team roster, current handoff points
More detail
  • Typical stages: sales handoff, onboarding, adoption, value realization, renewal, expansion.
  • Write exit criteria as customer outcomes, such as "first report delivered to their executive," not internal tasks.
2

Define success and health

Agree with each customer segment on what success means, then build a health score that predicts risk early enough to act on it.

What you need: Segment definitions, usage data, support history, the health score template below
3

Build escalation and SLA governance

Set severity levels, tier ownership, response and resolution targets, and a clear path to executive escalation. Then audit the backlog against it.

What you need: Ticketing platform, the escalation matrix template below, leadership sign-off on targets
More detail
  • When a queue is far behind, reach out to affected customers personally. Acknowledging a lapse rebuilds more trust than silence does.
  • Track SLA breaches weekly, not monthly. Breaches compound.
4

Instrument the reporting

Build one source of truth for retention, service, and value metrics. Automate it so the team spends its time acting on data, not assembling it.

What you need: CRM, BI tool, the metric definitions in this playbook
5

Enable the team

Give frontline and customer-facing teams runbooks, troubleshooting guides, and self-service content so good outcomes do not depend on who picks up the ticket.

What you need: Top ticket drivers, a knowledge base, time with your best performers
6

Run the value cadence

Hold regular business reviews, risk reviews, and expansion conversations. Customers renew what they can see, so make the value visible on a schedule.

What you need: The business review agenda below, account health data, executive sponsors on both sides

Customer health score framework

Use this when you need an early warning system for churn risk, or your current score is not predicting what actually happens.

Outcome progress            25%      Success criteria    Partially met       Not met or
                                     met on schedule                         undefined
Support experience          15%      No open P1/P2       Aging tickets       Open escalation
Relationship strength       15%      Exec sponsor        Champion only       No active
                                     engaged                                 contact
Sentiment (CSAT / survey)   15%      [>=90%]            [75-89%]            [<75%]

SCORING
Green = 3 points, Yellow = 2, Red = 1. Multiply by weight and total.
Overall: 2.5+ Healthy | 1.8-2.49 Watch | Below 1.8 At risk

REQUIRED ACTIONS
At risk: recovery plan within 5 business days, owner named, leadership visibility
Watch: CSM outreach within 10 business days, root cause noted in CRM
Healthy: identify expansion or advocacy opportunity at next review

CALIBRATION
Every quarter, compare scores from 90 days ago with actual renewals and churn.
If the score missed, change the weights. A health score is a hypothesis.

Escalation matrix and SLA governance

Use this when escalations depend on who is available, tickets age without owners, or customers escalate straight to your executives.

ESCALATION MATRIX AND SLA GOVERNANCE
Team / platform: [Team name, ticketing system]

SEVERITY DEFINITIONS
P1 Critical   Service down or data at risk for multiple customers
P2 High       Core workflow blocked for one customer, no workaround
P3 Medium     Degraded experience, workaround available
P4 Low        Question, request, or cosmetic issue

TIERS AND OWNERSHIP
Tier 1  Frontline support    Triage, known fixes, knowledge base
Tier 2  Specialists          Complex troubleshooting, configuration
Tier 3  Engineering/Product  Defects, platform changes
Exec    [Director or VP name]   P1s, at-risk accounts, customer executive requests

SLA TARGETS
Severity   First response   Update cadence     Resolution target
P1         [30 min]         [Every 2 hours]    [8 hours]
P2         [2 hours]        [Daily]            [2 business days]
P3         [1 business day] [Every 3 days]     [5 business days]
P4         [2 business days] [On change]       [10 business days]

RULES
1. Every ticket has one named owner at all times.
2. A breached SLA escalates automatically one tier up.
3. P1s trigger a customer communication within the first-response window.
4. Weekly review: breaches, oldest open tickets, repeat drivers.
5. Monthly: top three root causes go to Product with customer impact attached.

Executive business review agenda

Use this when you want business reviews that customers' executives actually attend, and that lead to renewal and expansion conversations.

EXECUTIVE BUSINESS REVIEW (45 minutes)
Customer: [Name]     Attendees: [Their exec, champion; our exec, CSM]

5 min   Their priorities
        Confirm what matters to them this quarter. Ask, do not assume.

10 min  Outcomes delivered
        Results against the success criteria agreed at onboarding,
        in their business terms: time saved, cost avoided, revenue supported.

10 min  What we learned
        Adoption trends, support themes, and one honest area to improve.

10 min  What comes next
        Recommendations tied to their priorities, including any expansion
        that solves a stated problem.

5 min   Commitments
        Named owners and dates on both sides.

5 min   Feedback
        How are we doing as a partner? What would make us indispensable?

FOLLOW-UP within 2 business days: summary, commitments, next review date.

AI prompt: customer operations analyst

I will paste [ticket data / survey responses / churn notes] covering [time period]. All personal data has been removed.

Analyze it and return:
1. The top five drivers of volume or dissatisfaction, with counts or share of total.
2. For each driver: likely root cause, whether it is a product, process, or people issue, and the evidence.
3. Leading indicators of churn risk you can see in the data.
4. Three fixes ranked by impact and effort, each with an owner function and a metric to track.
5. What the data cannot tell us, and what I should collect next.

Rules:
- Separate what the data shows from what you are inferring. Label inferences.
- Do not invent numbers. If something is not in the data, say so.
- Write for an executive audience: concise, specific, decision-ready.

Failure modes to design out

Most customer operations break in predictable places. Name the ones most likely in your organization and build a control for each.

Lifecycle

  • Onboarding with no agreed success criteria
  • Sales-to-CS handoff that loses context
  • Renewal work that starts 30 days out

Governance

  • Tickets with no single owner
  • Escalation that depends on who is online
  • Backlog nobody reviews by age

Data

  • Vanity metrics in executive reports
  • Health scores never checked against outcomes
  • Three teams, three versions of churn

People

  • Outcomes that depend on heroics
  • No runbooks for top ticket drivers
  • Frontline feedback that never reaches Product

Customer

  • Silence mistaken for satisfaction
  • Value delivered but never reported
  • Executive sponsor who left unnoticed

Principles behind the playbook

The ideas I come back to every time I build or rebuild a customer operation.

Customer success is an operating system, not a department

Retention is decided by how Sales, Product, Support, and Finance work together. A CS team alone cannot fix a broken handoff.

Design beats effort

If good outcomes depend on people working harder, the system is the problem. Build processes that make the right result the easy result.

Empathy is a strategy that scales

Understanding what customers expected, and saying plainly where you fell short, rebuilds trust faster than any discount.

One size does not fit all

Segment by what customers need to succeed, not only by what they pay. Different segments need different motions.

Silence is not satisfaction

Customers who stop complaining have often stopped caring. Measure engagement, not only the absence of tickets.

Make value visible

Customers renew what they can see. If you deliver outcomes but never report them, you have left the renewal to chance.
